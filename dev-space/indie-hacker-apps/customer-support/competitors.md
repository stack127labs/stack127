# Customer support directory competitor pages

Checked 2026-11-09. I sampled pages surfaced by searches for startup help desks, free live chat, help desk software, open-source help desks, and Intercom alternatives. Search rankings vary by place, device, and time; this is a useful sample, not a fixed ranking. No accounts were created and no forms were submitted.

## Pages reviewed

### 1. G2 Help Desk Software

- **URL and operator:** [G2 Help Desk Software](https://www.g2.com/categories/help-desk), run by G2, a software review and discovery marketplace.
- **Layout and app data:** A large category page with a satisfaction/market-presence grid, a small “Top Tools at a Glance” table, and long product profiles. The glance table shows product, best for, and one review title. Profiles add average rating, review count, review sub-scores, seller and company details, typical users and industries, company-size mix, AI summaries of pros and cons, recent review titles, and product links. The page says it tracks 657 products.
- **Filters and sorting:** The page text exposes the G2 Grid and links to related categories, but no clear filter or sort controls. I could not verify a live filter set from the readable page.
- **Price:** The at-a-glance table has no price column. The category guide describes common per-user monthly or annual prices and gives an average based on editions across the category. It does not give a comparable AI usage price or consistent starting price for each product on this page.
- **Sources and dates:** G2 says rankings use verified reviews and a stated scoring method. Product profiles link to sellers and reviews. The header says “Last updated: November 01, 2026,” while the embedded category guide says it was updated April 9, 2026. Those appear to date different page sections. [G2’s category page](https://www.g2.com/categories/help-desk)
- **What works / what is weak:** Review volume, user roles, company sizes, and reviewer pros/cons help buyers judge fit. The page leans toward review and market signals, not a quick cost or channel comparison. Its category includes internal IT and large-enterprise products, with little help for a solo buyer to narrow the list.

### 2. Capterra Help Desk Software

- **URL and operator:** [Capterra Help Desk Software](https://www.capterra.com/help-desk-software/), currently labeled “powered by G2 Digital Markets.” G2 says Capterra and its related discovery sites became part of G2 in early 2026. [Capterra’s vendor page](https://www.capterra.com/vendors/) · [G2’s acquisition update](https://company.g2.com/news/g2-expands-buyer-intent-capabilities)
- **Layout and app data:** A searchable category directory with product cards and editorial buying guidance. Cards show product name, star rating and review count, a “highly rated for” label, starting price and unit where published, feature ratings, reviewer pros/cons, a short description, and often trial/free-plan details. Product pages add feature lists and “add to compare.” Capterra’s [Live Chat Shortlist](https://www.capterra.com/live-chat-software/shortlist/) shows a comparison grid with Capterra score, starting price, free plan, free trial, standout feature, and integration count.
- **Filters and sorting:** Category paths expose filters such as free version, free trial, small business, and deployment; the live-chat page also labels a Filter control. The exact sort choices were not exposed in the page text I could retrieve, so I have not assumed them.
- **Price:** Usually a published starting figure with its stated basis, such as per user/month or flat rate/month; some cards say “not provided” or lead to “Get Price.” Free plans and trials are separate labels. AI resolution or message-credit costs are not a standard comparison field.
- **Sources and dates:** Product ratings and review counts are tied to user reviews; Capterra links its Shortlist scoring method. Pages are labeled for 2026, but the category cards do not show a price-checked date beside each app. [Capterra Help Desk Software](https://www.capterra.com/help-desk-software/)
- **What works / what is weak:** Filters, review dimensions, starting prices, and comparison actions make a broad catalog usable. Prices and review evidence are not equally complete for every product, and a “Get Price” button can replace a number. It does not normalize AI usage charges or show the likely bill for a one- or three-person team.

### 3. Toolradar: Best Help Desk for Startups

- **URL and operator:** [Best Help Desk for Startups](https://toolradar.com/guides/best-help-desk-for-startups), a Toolradar editorial guide. The page does not clearly identify a separate legal operator.
- **Layout and app data:** A long guide with a linked table of contents, a top-picks table, detailed product sections, and a second price comparison. The first table shows tool, starting price, rating/review count, and best for. The pricing table shows best for, what you pay now, price after a startup grant, and free plan. The detailed sections add eligibility limits, seats, AI allowances, and plan caveats.
- **Filters and sorting:** No product filters or sortable directory table. The contents links jump to sections; editorial ranks and labels make it a curated list.
- **Price:** It compares free plans, trials, time-limited startup grants, annual per-seat prices, flat workspace prices, minimum seats, and some AI/session costs. It makes the post-grant price visible instead of treating a temporary discount as a permanent free plan.
- **Sources and dates:** Updated September 2026. Toolradar says it checked prices on vendor pages that month, links many of those pages from the pricing table, and says it did not accept paid placement. It also explains its startup-specific selection method. [Guide and price table](https://toolradar.com/guides/best-help-desk-for-startups)
- **What works / what is weak:** It is unusually relevant to founders: eligibility, small-team caps, seat minimums, and “what happens after free” are part of the comparison. The list is still an editorial shortlist of 10 from its larger category, so coverage and “best for” judgments depend on its chosen startup scenario.

### 4. eesel AI: Best Helpdesk Tools for Startups

- **URL and operator:** [The best helpdesk tools for startups](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/list-of-helpdesk-tools-for-startups), published by eesel AI, which also sells a product included in its own list.
- **Layout and app data:** A long article with a contents list, one “at a glance” table, detailed product sections, and a real-cost example. The table shows tool, best for, free option, entry paid price, pricing model, native AI, and standout for startups. Product sections add what it does, pros, cons, pricing, and an editorial take. Its example models a three-agent team handling 500 tickets a month.
- **Filters and sorting:** No filters or sortable list; only the table of contents and section links.
- **Price:** It distinguishes per-seat, per-ticket/conversation, workspace, and fixed-credit models, and calls out AI charges such as per-resolution add-ons. Its scenario helps show how the unit changes the bill.
- **Sources and dates:** The article says it tried products, read vendor pricing pages, and reviewed Reddit and G2. Product names link out, but the at-a-glance table does not attach a source and price-check date to each value. No clear row-level evidence ledger is shown. [Article](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/list-of-helpdesk-tools-for-startups)
- **What works / what is weak:** The model column and cost example expose why headline prices are hard to compare. The publisher is also a listed competitor, so its inclusion and judgments need to be read with that interest in mind. The table does not provide channels, data location, hosting, or licensing consistently.

### 5. Zendesk: 16 Best Free Live Chat Software

- **URL and operator:** [16 Best Free Live Chat Software](https://www.zendesk.com/service/messaging/best-free-live-chat-software/), published by Zendesk, a vendor in the category.
- **Layout and app data:** A long article with a contents list, tool-by-tool feature summaries, and a comparison table. The table fields are software, starting price (described as per user/month), free trial or free plan, and key features. Individual sections list features, free-trial length, whether a continuing free plan exists, and pricing.
- **Filters and sorting:** No filters or sortable comparison. The contents links move through the article.
- **Price:** Mostly seat/agent/user per month, with exceptions such as annual bundles, workspace plans, flat monthly prices, free, or “contact sales.” It distinguishes free plan from free trial in the comparison, though the page title groups both under “free.” AI resolution charges are not a standard column.
- **Sources and dates:** Product names and images link to vendors, but no per-row source trail or price-checked date is shown in the article text. The search result labels the article as recent; the page itself does not give a clear date next to the comparison. [Article and comparison table](https://www.zendesk.com/service/messaging/best-free-live-chat-software/)
- **What works / what is weak:** It gives a quick scan of feature coverage and whether a tool has a free plan or trial, then adds enough detail to understand the basic setup. It is a vendor’s page, includes Zendesk among its picks, and mixes different pricing bases. “Free” can mean a permanent plan or a trial, and there is no team-size cost example.

### 6. Intercom: 10 Best Intercom Alternatives

- **URL and operator:** [10 Best Intercom Alternatives](https://www.intercom.com/learning-center/intercom-alternatives), on Intercom’s Learning Center and attributed to the Fin Team. This is a competitor’s own comparison page.
- **Layout and app data:** A long article with a contents list, individual product sections, and a comparison matrix. The matrix shows platform, best for, starting price, AI resolution model, native voice, and languages. Product sections add use case, starting price, AI capabilities, strengths, limitations, and when to choose it.
- **Filters and sorting:** No product filters or sorting; the table of contents links to article sections.
- **Price:** It explicitly mixes per-seat, per-resolution, per-conversation, per-session, AI add-on, free, and custom-price offers. The AI-resolution column is useful, but the starting-price column is not a standardized total-cost estimate.
- **Sources and dates:** The page is dated September 23, 2026, and has a “How We Evaluated” section. The matrix does not show a source or last-checked date next to each price; some figures are estimates. [Article and matrix](https://www.intercom.com/learning-center/intercom-alternatives)
- **What works / what is weak:** It compares AI billing and voice/language coverage, which many general lists omit, and states a use case for each app. It is a vendor-authored alternatives page and includes Intercom as a row; estimates and unsupported price cells make independent verification important.

### 7. Geekflare: Open Source Help Desk Software

- **URL and operator:** [10 Best Open Source Help Desk Software](https://geekflare.com/open-source/help-desk/), a Geekflare software guide.
- **Layout and app data:** A long guide with summary picks, hands-on setup findings, and a comparison table. The table shows rank, software, license, type, GitHub stars, latest release, Docker, SSO, and managed-cloud price. Product sections add version/stack, database, release date, pros/cons, and setup notes.
- **Filters and sorting:** The comparison table can be sorted by software, GitHub stars, and latest release. The page specifically points readers to sorting by release date. No fit, cost, or feature filters were apparent.
- **Price:** Self-hosted software is separated from managed cloud where available. Hosted prices are presented with a unit and billing period when found, often per agent/month billed yearly; products without a published hosted offer are marked accordingly. Server, maintenance, and labor costs are not included.
- **Sources and dates:** Updated October 19, 2026; data refreshed October 23. Geekflare says it tested several tools on a stated server setup and says release/license data comes from GitHub while editions, prices, and requirements come from project docs. It links a methodology. [Guide and methodology](https://geekflare.com/open-source/help-desk/)
- **What works / what is weak:** License, release freshness, self-host requirements, and cloud price sit together, helping readers avoid stale or mislicensed projects. It only covers open-source/self-hostable tools, and its performance results come from one specified server setup; the page does not cover commercial hosted tools broadly or data residency.

## What nearly all of these pages show

- The app name and a short use-case label.
- A headline price, free option, or trial, although the unit and conditions are often inconsistent.
- A short feature summary or a “best for” label.
- Some way to establish trust: user ratings/reviews, editorial picks, or a testing/methodology note.

General directories are strongest on review volume, feature ratings, and browsing a large catalog. Specialist lists are stronger on a particular buyer’s situation, such as a startup grant, free chat, or self-hosting.

## What they do not put together that our data has

None of these seven pages presents a consistent, source-linked record for every app covering both seat cost and AI usage cost, supported channels, branding removal, self-hosted options, data location, and current product changes such as sales, rebrands, or new-signup routes. There are partial exceptions: Geekflare shows license and hosted options for open-source projects, and several pages show some channel or AI information. No page brings the full set together with the same units, source dates, and lifecycle notes across hosted and self-hosted products.

## Five ideas for our directory page

1. **Put a compact comparison table first.** Show fit, hosted/self-hosted, channels, a free-plan or trial label, seat price, AI billing unit, and the next meaningful limit. Let people scan before opening app cards.
2. **Make price comparable without hiding its basis.** Keep the vendor’s currency, billing period, seat/workspace/ticket/resolution unit, and plan conditions. Add our clearly labeled one-person and three-person examples, plus an AI-usage example where it changes the bill.
3. **Offer filters around real solo-builder choices.** Include free forever vs trial, per-seat vs flat vs usage pricing, self-hosted/open source, core channels, minimum seats, and whether branding can be removed. Do not make “has AI” the only AI filter; show how it is billed.
4. **Make evidence and freshness visible on each app.** Link each price, policy, hosting, and lifecycle fact to the official page and show its check date. Flag acquisition, sunset, pivot, signup routing, and legacy-customer changes near the app name.
5. **Organize around fit, not one universal winner.** Let readers narrow to solo founder, small SaaS, ecommerce, social messaging, live chat, AI-first support, or self-hosted. Explain important trade-offs and exceptions rather than ranking every product on one score.
