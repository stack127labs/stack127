# Competitor pages: databases and backend

Checked 2026-11-09. This review looks at seven pages that appeared in searches for database software, Firebase alternatives, free database tiers, database rankings, or object storage pricing. The pages cover different jobs: browse a large directory, find an alternative, inspect database technology, compare a free allowance, read an editorial shortlist, or estimate a storage bill. Search positions change by place and time; I did not use a rank-tracking service or claim a fixed position.

I read the public pages and linked methodology/about pages. I did not sign up or submit forms. “Works well” and “weak” are editorial judgments about helping a solo developer choose a product.

## Pages reviewed

### 1. G2 — Database as a Service providers

- **Page:** [Best Database as a Service (DBaaS) Providers](https://www.g2.com/categories/database-as-a-service-dbaas)
- **Who runs it:** G2.com, Inc.; G2’s [terms](https://legal.g2.com/terms-of-use) name the company that operates G2 and its related sites.
- **Layout:** A long category page. It combines a satisfaction-versus-market-presence grid, ranked product writeups, review snippets, and small “top tools” tables. The current page says it covers 141 products and was last updated October 25, 2026.
- **What it shows per product:** Name and description; rating and review count; selected performance, ease-of-use, scalability, and provisioning scores; seller/company details; likely user roles and company sizes; AI summaries of review pros and cons; recent review titles and dates. The at-a-glance table adds “best for” and one review quote.
- **Filters and sorting:** The page groups products into categories and ranks them using G2 scores. Its wider comparison flow says you can compare up to four products and filter by company size. The category landing page itself is not a filter-first directory and does not expose a useful set of developer-specific filters.
- **Price:** No normalized price column on this page. Price is discussed in review summaries or needs to be checked on individual product pages or vendor sites.
- **Sources and dates:** G2 explains that rankings use moderated user reviews and a stated research method. The category has an update date; individual reviews have dates. It does not attach an official pricing or technical source URL and check date to each product fact.
- **What works well:** Large coverage, real buyer feedback, review volume, and category-specific rankings make it easy to shortlist known vendors. The review pros and cons can reveal setup or billing pain that a vendor feature list will not.
- **What is weak or missing:** It is built around organization buying and review signals, not solo-app requirements. It mixes database engines, cloud platforms, analytics services, and DBaaS. It does not compare free allowances, usage meters, hard limits, regions, export paths, self-host editions, or lifecycle notices side by side. A star rating cannot tell a reader whether a project sleeps or what 100 GB of egress costs.

### 2. AlternativeTo — Firebase alternatives

- **Page:** [Firebase alternatives](https://alternativeto.net/software/firebase/)
- **Who runs it:** 27 Kilobyte AB, a Swedish company. Its [about page](https://alternativeto.net/about/) describes the recommendation lists as community-sourced; its [terms](https://alternativeto.net/about/terms/) say it does not test, verify, or certify most listed software.
- **Layout:** An alternatives page with a ranked list of products. Each entry has a short description and metadata, with screenshots and community comments for some items. The page says it was last updated July 18, 2026.
- **What it shows per product:** Position, likes, description, price model (such as free or freemium), open-source/proprietary label, application type, supported platforms, country label, screenshots, and comments. Some entries show a direct “X vs Firebase” comparison.
- **Filters and sorting:** Its list supports filtering by platform and license; for example, the [open-source Supabase alternatives view](https://alternativeto.net/software/supabase/?license=opensource) applies an open-source filter. Alternatives are ordered using community popularity/likes. The page does not provide database-specific filters such as SQL model, backup, region, or scale-to-zero.
- **Price:** A broad label such as Free or Freemium, not a plan price or unit rate.
- **Sources and dates:** The page has a last-updated date and dated user comments. Entries link to product and license pages, but feature and price labels do not carry a source quote or checked date. Its terms say listings are community submissions and are not tested or certified by the site.
- **What works well:** “What can replace Firebase?” is a clear path into the list. Platform and license filters help people find a self-hosted or open-source option, and votes surface well-known alternatives.
- **What is weak or missing:** The broad alternative label can put a complete backend, a database engine, and a small self-hosted server beside each other without explaining feature gaps. Free/Freemium does not say what the free tier includes. No per-plan limits, current rates, required region, data export/deletion terms, or dated product news appear in the list.

### 3. DB-Engines — database popularity ranking

- **Page:** [DB-Engines Ranking](https://db-engines.com/en/ranking)
- **Who runs it:** Redgate Software maintains the site. Redgate [announced its acquisition](https://www.red-gate.com/our-company/newsroom/press-releases/redgate-software-announces-acquisition-of-db-engines/) of DB-Engines in 2024; it kept the DB-Engines name and ranking.
- **Layout:** A sortable-looking ranking table with linked database profiles and historical trend charts. Separate views cover database-model categories, open-source versus commercial popularity, and trends.
- **What it shows per system:** Current rank, rank in prior periods, system name, database model, popularity score, and score change versus earlier months. The profile links add a technology description and related database facts.
- **Filters and sorting:** The main list is ordered by score/rank, with search and model-specific ranking pages. Readers can open trend charts or category and license-comparison views. It does not filter by free tier, provider region, app framework, or budget.
- **Price:** No service price or free-plan detail. “Open source vs. commercial” is a classification view, not a price comparison.
- **Sources and dates:** The ranking is updated monthly and states its period. Its [method page](https://db-engines.com/en/ranking_definition) lists search results, Google Trends, Q&A activity, jobs, professional profiles, and social mentions as popularity signals. It explicitly says the score does not measure installations or actual use inside IT systems. The method is public, but the ranking table does not cite each vendor fact.
- **What works well:** Broad technology coverage, monthly movement, model categories, and a transparent explanation of what its score means make it useful for understanding industry attention.
- **What is weak or missing:** Popularity is not fit, performance, or reliability. The ranking includes commercial engines and self-hosted projects, but does not compare their hosted plans, exact billing meters, free-tier traps, regions, backups, or migration costs. Its current Redgate ownership is useful context for trust, but the monthly score is still not an app-selection guide.

### 4. Database of Databases (DBDB.io) — browse and system records

- **Page:** [Browse database systems](https://dbdb.io/browse) and a representative [Supabase record](https://dbdb.io/db/supabase)
- **Who runs it:** An academic project of the Carnegie Mellon Database Group. The [about page](https://dbdb.io/about) says students contribute research as part of database courses.
- **Layout:** A large searchable catalog with an advanced-search builder and configurable result columns, plus structured profiles and revision history. The browse page currently lists more than 1,200 systems.
- **What it shows per system:** The default browse table shows name, start year, and data model. Readers can add fields such as developer, acquisition, country, license, hosted services, operating system, supported languages, storage architecture, query interface, and implementation features. Profiles add technical descriptions, source-code and documentation links, citations, history, related systems, and hosted-by relationships.
- **Filters and sorting:** Advanced search can combine product attributes and technical fields, including data model, developer, license, project type, hosted by, architecture, indexes, and query interface. Columns are configurable and searches can be saved. The standard browse list is alphabetical; separate leaderboards sort by recent edits, views, and edits.
- **Price:** No free-tier, plan, or metered price comparison. It describes software and hosting relationships, not the current cost of running each service.
- **Sources and dates:** Records include citations and revisions. The catalog’s recent-changes page exposes edit dates; the About page has its own last-updated date. Source depth varies by entry, so a profile needs checking before relying on an individual claim.
- **What works well:** It is the strongest technical reference in this set for model, implementation, license, developer, acquisition, and hosting relationships. Searchable feature fields and citations make it possible to investigate unusual systems without forcing everything into a prose roundup.
- **What is weak or missing:** It is an encyclopedia of database systems, not a buyer’s guide to managed backend services. It does not compare current free allowances, paid usage meters, hard limits, exact cloud regions, idle charges, export/deletion rules, or incidents across vendors. A technical profile may also need a separate hosted-product record.

### 5. TierDiff — free database tiers

- **Page:** [Database free tiers side by side](https://tierdiff.com/c/database/)
- **Who runs it:** An independent tracker. Its [about page](https://tierdiff.com/about/) says it is not owned by the services it lists; it does not name an operator there.
- **Layout:** A compact, sortable table with a source/evidence expansion per provider. A separate [SQL database picks page](https://tierdiff.com/best/database-sql/) turns selected free limits into a use-case score.
- **What it shows per service:** Database size, number of projects/databases, bandwidth out, file storage, reads/operations, monthly active users, request counts, and a link to the source. A dash marks a number the page did not publish. Evidence shows the copied vendor sentence and check date.
- **Filters and sorting:** A sort selector and copy-table action are visible. The page is already scoped to database services; it does not expose a broad set of reader-selected database, hosting-mode, or region filters. The related “best SQL” page ranks a fixed set by its published formula.
- **Price:** The comparison focuses on the $0 plan and its included allowance. The related ranking can include a cheapest paid price where enough services have a sourced monthly price; it is a limited price signal, not a bill calculator.
- **Sources and dates:** The [methodology](https://tierdiff.com/methodology/) says a bot checks public pricing/docs pages daily, stores the exact sentence, URL, and date, treats fetch failures separately, and requires human confirmation before a change enters history. The page snapshot opened for this review displayed an October 17, 2026 check date; methodology says the tracker checks daily.
- **What works well:** It presents free allowances in comparable units, links each number to vendor evidence, makes unknowns visible, and explains its scoring. This is a strong pattern for trust and for answering “what can I build without paying?”
- **What is weak or missing:** It covers a narrow free-tier slice rather than the full recurring bill. It does not fully show overage tiers, idle billing, region rates, export and deletion, self-host requirements, or product news. A metric-only score can also miss a qualitative blocker, so the scoring formula and missing-value treatment must stay visible.

### 6. TechRadar — best cloud databases

- **Page:** [Best cloud database of 2026](https://www.techradar.com/best/best-cloud-databases)
- **Who runs it:** TechRadar is owned by Future PLC, as its [about page](https://www.techradar.com/news/about-us) states.
- **Layout:** A long editorial shortlist with jump links for “best overall,” security, performance, scalability, and data management. Each pick has a short verdict, product explanation, strengths and drawbacks, and a vendor link.
- **What it shows per product:** A best-for label, broad features and intended workload, a few stated pros/cons, setup and scaling observations, and occasional price or free-tier notes. It does not use one consistent data schema across all picks.
- **Filters and sorting:** No interactive filters or table sorting. The page offers section jump links; editors choose and rank the picks.
- **Price:** Narrative descriptions such as pay-as-you-go or “contact sales,” plus selected plan/free-tier amounts. It does not calculate one identical workload across providers or break out all usage meters.
- **Sources and dates:** The page identifies a byline and says it was last updated March 12, 2026; its testing section describes broad evaluation criteria. It links to vendors and discloses affiliate commissions, but does not provide a source URL and checked date alongside each price or technical fact.
- **What works well:** Editorial categories and plain-language pros/cons help a reader start with a use case instead of a raw list. The narrative explains why a provider might fit a workload.
- **What is weak or missing:** The page is broad and enterprise-oriented, not aimed at a side project. Long-form copy can age between updates, and non-standard prices are hard to compare. It omits precise free-tier conditions, request/compute meters, region-specific rates, self-host/license differences, exit paths, and recent lifecycle or security events.

### 7. c17.io — object storage pricing comparison

- **Page:** [Object storage pricing, normalized](https://c17.io/compare/object-storage)
- **Who runs it:** An independent public software catalog built by an individual maintainer; the [about page](https://c17.io/about) describes the site and its data but does not identify a company operator.
- **Layout:** A detailed provider comparison table followed by three sample monthly cost tables and explanatory notes. It compares managed object storage and includes self-hosted examples.
- **What it shows per provider:** Storage price per GB-month, egress per GB, request fees, free tier/minimum, API compatibility, transfer quota, minimum duration/object size, plan notes, and region. The second table compares fixed small, medium, and large workloads with stated storage, egress, and request assumptions.
- **Filters and sorting:** A selector changes the comparison emphasis, including cheaper/more expensive ordering. The worked workloads are fixed examples rather than a user-entered calculator; there are no broad technical feature filters.
- **Price:** USD, with tax and unit assumptions stated. It shows normalized unit prices and estimated totals for stated workloads. The page says estimates are not guarantees, excludes unverified values, and links provider details.
- **Sources and dates:** It says its prices come from provider pages and shows a checked date of October 17, 2026. The comparison notes include source links and distinguish published, unverified, and unpublished values. Its [about page](https://c17.io/about) explains that the public catalog is produced from its own tool catalog.
- **What works well:** It makes egress, request charges, minimum storage, and included transfer visible beside storage price. The same-workload examples show why a low headline rate can still produce a high bill. Including self-hosted examples makes software cost versus hosting cost legible.
- **What is weak or missing:** Results depend on one selected region and hand-set workload assumptions; they are estimates, not vendor quotes. The table cannot represent every storage class or special condition. It does not cover lifecycle events, data export/deletion, or a complete security and service-level comparison.

## What nearly every page shows

- Product or system names, short descriptions, and a way to reach the vendor or a fuller record.
- Some organizing signal: category, data model, user votes, popularity score, rating, best-for label, or a filtered use case.
- A few high-level commercial signals, such as free/freemium status, vendor pricing language, or a headline unit rate.
- Enough context to create a shortlist, but rarely enough to estimate the bill or confirm that an option can run the reader’s app in the desired place.

## What the reviewed pages do not bring together

Some pages handle one part well: DBDB.io has technical fields and citations; TierDiff has quoted free-tier evidence; c17.io compares storage fees; DB-Engines covers ownership/popularity history. **None of these seven pages brings the full decision set together per product and edition:**

- A lasting free tier versus a trial, credits, or free software that still needs paid hosting; exact included amounts and reset periods.
- Metered rates and the bill drivers beside the allowances, including overage tiers, minimums, idle compute, requests, egress, tax notes, and a stated workload estimate.
- Hard limits and plan restrictions for connections, projects, storage, queries, payloads, or concurrency.
- Region availability and data location tied to the plan and billable component, including the difference between a selectable region and a placement hint.
- Hosted and self-hosted editions separated, with exact licenses, feature gaps, dependencies, and a clear note that self-hosted software still needs infrastructure and operations.
- Export formats, backups, deletion timing, and the friction or cost of moving away.
- Dated lifecycle and trust news such as a sale, shutdown, signups closing, a retired paid feature, a customer-data breach, or regulatory action, linked to its source.
- A source URL and checked date attached to every key fact, including unknowns. A product page’s “updated” date is not the same as a dated source for each price and limit.

This is the useful distinction for Stack127: avoid a “best database” winner badge. Make it easy to compare a specific job, plan, region, and deployment mode, then show the evidence and any caveat that could change the choice.

## Five ideas for the Stack127 page

1. **Start with the job and mode.** Let readers choose database/backend, object storage, vector search, or queues/workflows; then filter hosted, self-hosted, or both. Keep an app’s hosted service and open-source edition as separate comparable offers.
2. **Put free and paid costs in one view.** Show whether the free offer lasts, its exact quotas and inactivity rules, then show the smallest relevant paid step and the actual overage meters. Add a few transparent side-project workloads with a formula and per-region inputs.
3. **Make limits and location first-class filters.** Filter by data model, API/runtime, max size or connections, region, and whether a region is guaranteed. Show product limits next to the relevant plan instead of burying them in a separate docs link.
4. **Show the exit path and operating burden.** Compare export/backup formats, deletion timing, license, self-host requirements, and known hosted-only features. This lets someone weigh convenience against lock-in and maintenance work.
5. **Build trust into every card.** Put official source links and checked dates beside price, limit, and lifecycle claims. Add a dated warning for acquisitions, closures, breaches, and retired features, with a short explanation of who is affected.
