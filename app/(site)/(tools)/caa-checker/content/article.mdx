## How does CAA find the policy for a name?

It uses the closest name that has CAA records. The check starts at the exact hostname and moves up one level at a time. The first name with records holds the whole policy, and anything higher up is ignored.

1. `www.shop.example.com` has no CAA records.
2. `shop.example.com` has no CAA records.
3. `example.com` has CAA records. This is the policy.

If no name up to the top-level domain has CAA records, CAA doesn't restrict anyone.

If a name is an alias (a CNAME), its target's CAA records count. The search doesn't climb the target's parent domains. It goes back to the parents of the name you entered. [RFC 8659](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8659.html#section-3) defines this search.

## What do issue, issuewild, and iodef mean?

<Terms>
<Term name="issue">Allows one certificate authority to issue normal certificates. With no `issuewild`, it covers wildcards too.</Term>
<Term name="issuewild">Allows one certificate authority to issue wildcard certificates, such as `*.example.com`. When any `issuewild` exists, wildcards ignore `issue`.</Term>
<Term name="iodef">An email or web address for reports about blocked requests. It doesn't allow or block anything.</Term>
<Term name='issue ";"'>An empty value. On its own, it allows no certificate authority.</Term>
<Term name="Flag 128">Marks a record as critical. A certificate authority that doesn't understand it must refuse to issue.</Term>
</Terms>

Several `issue` records add up, and each one allows one more certificate authority. A policy with only `issuewild` records leaves normal certificates unrestricted.

Use the value your certificate authority publishes for CAA, such as `letsencrypt.org` for [Let's Encrypt](https://letsencrypt.org/docs/caa/) or `digicert.com` for [DigiCert](https://docs.digicert.com/en/certcentral/manage-certificates/dns-caa-resource-record-check.html).

## Why was my certificate blocked by CAA?

These are the usual causes:

- A parent domain has CAA records you forgot, and your certificate authority isn't in them.
- You listed one certificate authority, but your host or CDN gets certificates from another. Check its docs before you restrict issuance.
- The certificate is a wildcard, and `issuewild` doesn't list your certificate authority.
- The CAA lookup itself fails. The result then says the policy couldn't be determined. [DNS lookup](/dns-lookup) explains SERVFAIL and other errors, and the [DNSSEC checker](/dnssec-checker) finds broken signatures, one possible cause.

Being allowed by CAA doesn't mean a certificate will be issued. The certificate authority still checks that you control the domain. This check reads the policy only and doesn't list certificates already issued.

The check asks Cloudflare's public resolver from stack127's server. stack127 doesn't keep the names you check.
