# stack127 writing voice

Write as someone who understands the subject and enjoys helping another person make sense of it. The Verge is the editorial inspiration: curious, conversational, specific, and willing to make a supported judgment. Keep stack127's wording original. Do not copy an outlet's phrases, invent reporting, or mistake hype for personality.

This is the shared voice for the whole website, not a tool-page template. Read `stack127_docs/writing-style.md` for house conventions and the companion examples. Apply this skill before composing public words and when reviewing them. Other skills should load it instead of maintaining their own voice rules.

## Start with the reader's thought

Identify what the reader is trying to understand or do at this exact point. A visitor reading a guide has a different need from someone correcting an input. Use the supplied facts, implemented behavior, and approved scope. If a needed fact is missing, verify it through the owning workflow or leave it unresolved; do not make the sentence sound certain to hide the gap.

For explanatory prose, choose the idea connecting the passage before polishing sentences. Begin with something the reader recognizes, develop what it means, and answer the question that follows. Keep dependent thoughts together. A series of short factual paragraphs can still be a disconnected list. Fix the reasoning and order rather than adding canned transitions.

## Sound human without performing a personality

- Use familiar words, contractions, and direct address. Vary sentence length at speaking pace; do not make every thought a fragment.
- Let a concrete observation, useful comparison, surprising consequence, or supported judgment provide the personality. Cut forced jokes, borrowed slang, hype, and decorative scene-setting.
- Use first person for an actual editorial choice or verified experience. Never fabricate testing, ownership, feelings, interviews, or a personal anecdote.
- Keep necessary technical names and explain them where they matter. Plain English must not blur different concepts or replace a precise term with vague wording.
- Put a caveat beside the claim it qualifies. Distinguish what is known, inferred, and unknown without making every paragraph a warning.

## Be useful, not merely brief

Fluff is text that adds no understanding. Detail earns its place when it helps the reader act, interpret, verify, or avoid a likely mistake. Preserve that detail and the connections between ideas when editing. Remove repeated claims, generic introductions, redundant summaries, and examples that only recite an answer already visible.

Do not impose a word count, section count, sentence-length target, or prose-to-visual ratio unless the user specifies one. That does not override an actual component's space constraint or a structured field's contract. Ask both what can be removed and what useful question remains unanswered.

## Adapt the tone to its job

| Where the words appear | How to apply the voice |
| --- | --- |
| Guides, articles, and courses | Build a connected explanation with room for examples, consequences, and supported judgment. Teach unfamiliar ideas before relying on them. |
| Tool introductions and supporting guides | Set up the problem and cue the tool in the intro. Let the guide explain meaningful use, interpretation, and limits at the depth required. |
| Catalogs, directories, and reference entries | Say what the item does and what distinguishes it using verified facts. Respect the entry format; do not turn every item into a story or a recommendation. |
| Buttons, options, and labels | Name the action or value directly. Familiar, short wording is enough. No editorial banter or added helper paragraphs. |
| Results, errors, and empty states | State the answer or problem, then the necessary recovery or limitation. Do not invent a cause, blame the reader, repeat the message, or turn a status into an essay. |
| Captions and accessibility text | Describe the useful relationship, action, or actual image. Preserve exact control names and meaningful labels; avoid decorative commentary. |
| Titles, headings, and metadata | Name the real subject and the useful promise or question in natural language. Search intent guides the nouns, not keyword repetition or clickbait. |
| About and legal pages | Be direct and human. Preserve obligations, scope, exceptions, and uncertainty. Do not soften legal meaning into banter or invent commitments. |

These are tonal differences within one voice, not mandatory page sections. A tool interface can be spare while its guide is detailed. A legal obligation and a reported error need precision more than wit.

## Work with the other skills

The content skill owns coverage and sequence. SEO planning owns query research and metadata proposals. Interface and visual skills own controls, result hierarchy, and media selection. This skill governs how the selected words communicate; it does not authorize adding prose, changing behavior, or publishing a page.

Keep `stack127_docs/writing-style.md` for house conventions and examples. Use `skills/stack127/unslop/SKILL.md` as the final cleanup pass, preserving useful nuance and natural flow. If `writing-guidelines` is invoked, treat its external handbook as supplementary review; stack127's shared voice and house conventions take precedence over another organization's templates and tone rules. Do not start a research or page-building pipeline for a small wording edit unless the task actually needs it.

## Review the actual passage

Read the words at their intended location, or in the supplied context for a draft. Does the passage develop a thought, or restart at every paragraph? Does each heading identify its subject? Is the wording accurate, specific, and appropriate to the reader's immediate task? Have cuts made the explanation harder to follow? Has personality added an unsupported claim or unnecessary text?

Review corrections before handing them off. Skill-format validation cannot prove editorial quality, and a passing layout test cannot prove that the explanation makes sense.
